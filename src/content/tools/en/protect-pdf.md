## When to password-protect a PDF

Email attachments get forwarded, cloud links get shared, and laptops get lost. A password means the document stays unreadable to anyone who does not have it. Typical cases:

- **Tax returns, payslips and bank statements** sent to an accountant or a landlord.
- **Medical records and HR documents.**
- **Contracts and offers** before they are signed.
- **Client deliverables** sent over email.

## Choosing a strong password

AES-256 cannot realistically be broken by brute force — but a weak password can be guessed. A few rules:

- Use **at least 12 characters**, or a passphrase of four or more unrelated words.
- Avoid names, birthdays and words related to the document.
- **Never send the password in the same email** as the PDF. Text it, say it on the phone or use a password manager's sharing feature.

## What a password does — and doesn't do

- **It encrypts the content.** Without the password, the text and images of the PDF cannot be read, even by opening the file in another program.
- **It doesn't stop sharing.** Anyone you give the password to can open the PDF, save it, print it or forward it together with the password.
- **It doesn't hide the file name.** Name the file neutrally (for example `document-2026.pdf` rather than `salary-john-smith.pdf`) if the name itself is sensitive.

## Common problems and fixes

**The recipient says the password doesn't work.** Passwords are case-sensitive and spaces count. Avoid characters that are hard to type on a phone keyboard, and send the password as plain text rather than in a formatted message where quotes or dashes may change.

**I need to edit a protected PDF.** Unlock it with [Unlock PDF](/unlock-pdf) using its password, make your changes, then protect it again.

**An upload portal rejects the PDF.** Many official portals cannot read encrypted files. Upload the unprotected version directly to the portal, and keep password protection for files you send by email or share by link.
