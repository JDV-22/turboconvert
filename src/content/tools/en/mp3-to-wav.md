## When you need WAV

WAV is the standard uncompressed audio format. It's less practical than MP3 for listening — files are big — but it's the format many tools work with best:

- **Audio and video editors** — editing a WAV avoids decoding an MP3 at every step and prevents piling one lossy compression on top of another when you export.
- **Samplers, drum machines and DJ hardware** that only read WAV.
- **Burning an audio CD** with software that expects uncompressed files.
- **Phone systems, on-hold messages and voice prompts** that require WAV uploads.
- **Games, apps and interactive projects** that use WAV for sound effects.
- **Speech recognition and analysis tools** that accept WAV more reliably than other formats.

## MP3 vs WAV

| | MP3 | WAV |
|---|---|---|
| Compression | Lossy | None |
| Size per minute | ≈ 1–2.4 MB | ≈ 10 MB (CD quality) |
| Editing without extra loss | No | Yes |
| Best for | Listening, sharing | Editing, production, hardware |

## Good to know about quality

Converting to WAV freezes the audio in its current state. If the MP3 was encoded at 128 kbps, the WAV will sound exactly like a 128 kbps MP3 — just in a bigger file. For the best result, start from the highest-quality source you have (a FLAC or the original recording if available).

Several files can be converted at once — handy when preparing a set of samples or a full album for a CD.

## Common problems

- **The WAV is huge.** Expected: about 10 MB per minute. Use FLAC (via the [audio converter](/audio-converter)) if you need lossless audio at around half the size and your software reads FLAC.
- **A device or phone system rejects the WAV.** Some systems require a specific sample rate or mono audio; check the requirements and use dedicated audio software if they're unusual.
