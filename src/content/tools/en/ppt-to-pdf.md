## Why convert slides to PDF?

PowerPoint files are big, need compatible software and can look different from one computer to another. A PDF solves all three:

- **Sharing after a talk** — attendees can open the PDF on any device.
- **Handouts and printing** — one slide per page, ready for the printer.
- **Submitting coursework or proposals** — many platforms accept PDF only.
- **Sending to clients** — your design arrives as you made it and cannot be edited by accident.

## What is kept, what is not

| Element | In the PDF |
|---|---|
| Text, shapes, images, backgrounds | Rendered on each page |
| Slide order and size | Same as in your presentation |
| Fonts | Used when available, otherwise replaced by a close match |
| Animations and transitions | Not kept — each slide is shown fully built |
| Embedded video and audio | Not kept |

## Tips

- **Use common fonts** for decks that must look exact elsewhere; unusual fonts are substituted.

## Common problems and fixes

**"Not a supported file."** The presentation is probably an old .ppt, a Keynote file or an OpenDocument .odp. Export or save it as .pptx first — Keynote and Google Slides both offer *Download / Export as PowerPoint*.

**Text overflows its box.** A replacement font can be a little wider than the original. Switch the deck to a common font, or shorten the longest lines, and convert again.

**A slide with animations looks crowded.** Every element that appears during the animation is shown at once in the PDF. If objects were meant to replace each other, duplicate the slide in PowerPoint and keep one state per slide.

**My video is missing.** PDFs cannot embed playable video. Put a screenshot of the video and its link on the slide, so readers of the PDF can still watch it.

**The PDF is too big to email.** High-resolution photos in slides are the usual cause. Compress the PDF, or use *Compress Pictures* in PowerPoint before converting.
